How I approach a screen

Three habits that show up in every project below.

Start from the decision

Every screen exists to help someone decide something: enrol, continue, upgrade. I design around that decision first and arrange everything else to support it.

Design every state

The new user with no data, the returning user mid-task, the error. The happy path is the easy part. The other states are where products feel broken or considered.

Hand off something buildable

I work with developers from wireframes onwards, so components, breakpoints and motion are agreed before the final screens, not argued about after.

In-vehicle display UX

A centre-display system for a car: home dashboard, navigation, media, phone, apps and settings. I designed it in three visual directions, all built on the same structure, so a brand can change the look without relearning the layout.

Platform
Centre display, 16:9 and ultra-wide 8:3
Scope
Information architecture, UI and visual design
Screens
Six core screens in two directions, plus a wide home screen in light and dark
Directions
Sand, Blue and Glass

Built for a glance

The number a driver needs most is always the biggest thing on screen: speed on the home screen, distance to the next turn in navigation, the call timer and end-call button on a call.

Controls where the hand is

A fixed rail on the driver's side, or a bottom dock on the wide layout, keeps all six destinations one tap away from any screen. Volume stays in the dock on the wide screen, so it never needs a menu.

One system, three looks

Every direction shares the same structure and components; only colour, wallpaper and density change. Settings let drivers set wallpaper, text size, brightness, a dark-mode schedule and the colour of the ambient lighting.

Sand

A warm, low-glare palette with landscape wallpapers and an orange accent. The home screen brings speed, driver-assist status, the next turn, media and dual-zone climate into one view.

Blue

A cool daylight direction with a blue accent and a status bar on every screen. Metric units, a speed-limit badge, range and tyre pressure on the home screen, recent calls beside the active call, and ambient-lighting colours in settings.

Glass

An ultra-wide layout for a full-width display, with translucent panels, a bottom dock and an FM radio tuning dial. The same screen in light and dark.

Prank Time

My own Android app, published under my Pixel Dass name. It shows your friends a fake clock: set a time hours ahead, hand the phone over and watch them panic about being late. I designed and built it end to end: concept, user flows, interface, app icon, store graphics and the app itself, through to publishing it on Google Play.

Type
Personal product, Android
Role
Concept, UX, UI, branding and development
Status
Live on Google Play, 100+ downloads
Publisher
Pixel Dass
View on Google Play

Their own screen, a fake time

The most convincing prank uses the victim's real home screen. The app hints at it right on the home screen: take a screenshot, upload it, and the fake clock sits on top. Or pick one of the built-in wallpapers instead.

29 clock styles

Digital and analogue faces modelled on real phones, from Date Classic and Stacked to Glass, Ring and Split Date, so the fake clock matches what the phone would normally show.

Pick the moment

The settings screen separates the time the clock shows from when the prank pops up, each with its own hour and minute fields. Duration is a row of one-tap presets rather than another picker, and pranks can repeat on chosen days.

Works on the lock screen

At the set moment the fake clock fills the screen, even on the lock screen, so it looks like the phone itself is showing the wrong time.

Learn it in four steps

A short "How it works" tour explains the idea in four illustrated steps, with Skip always available, and ends on one clear action: "Let's prank!"

A playful identity

A winking alarm-clock character, purple-to-orange gradients and glossy, toy-like buttons. It looks like a toy because it is one, while the controls stay large and easy to hit.

How the screens evolved

Two screens in their first version and as they ship today.

Prank settings

The first version used scroll-wheel pickers in long pill rows and a two-number duration. Today it uses separate hour and minute fields with AM/PM toggles, one-tap duration presets and grouped sections, in a glossier visual style.

Home

The first home was just an upload box and two buttons. Today it adds a brand header, a help button that reopens the tour, and a hint suggesting a screenshot of the victim's own home screen.

Tiltopia

My second app: an emoji block puzzle with a twist. Place blocks to clear rows and columns, and every clear tilts the board, sliding the remaining blocks into new chains and combos. I designed and built it end to end: game concept, rules and level design, interface, characters, store graphics and the Android app.

Type
Personal product, mobile game
Role
Concept, game design, UX, UI, art and development
Platform
Android, plus a web version
Status
Launching soon on Google Play

One rule that changes everything

Block puzzles are a crowded genre, so the game is built around a single new idea: clear a line and the board tilts. A "Next tilt" arrow shows which way it will slide, so players can plan chain reactions instead of just filling gaps.

Readable at a glance

Score, best, coins and the next tilt sit in a fixed top bar, and boosters (Bomb, Flip tilt, Shuffle, Undo) sit in a fixed bottom bar with counts on each. Big callouts like "Chain x3!" and "Amazing!" reward combos without hiding the board.

A world to progress through

An adventure map leads through themed worlds of emojis (fruit, ocean, bugs, sport, space and more) with gems to collect and up to three stars per level, so every session moves the player forward.

Reasons to come back

A daily challenge gives everyone the same puzzle each day, alongside missions, a day streak, a daily chest, a shop and stats, all one tap from the main menu.

Learn by doing

The tutorial teaches with the real board: one tip, one gap, a hand showing where to drag. Skip is always there, and the first win comes within seconds.

Characters with personality

Glossy, squishy block characters (a smiling yellow cube, a heart-eyed pink one, a winking blue one) give the game a mascot set and carry through the icon, store art and celebrations.

Case studies

Platform UX and SVG animation system

Log2Base2 promises to teach programming visually, but its static, disconnected screens didn't live up to that promise. I redesigned the platform's UX and built a set of animated SVGs that explain concepts right on the page.

Client
Log2Base2
Role
Senior designer, product leadership team
Scope
Information architecture, UI, SVG animation
Tools
Adobe XD, Photoshop, SVGator

The problem

The platform's product is visual learning, yet the interface was a collection of static, unrelated components. It felt generic, and it undercut the one thing that set the product apart.

Objectives

Bring structure and motion together so that animation explains rather than decorates. Keep the system coherent and fast across a large, growing content library.

Approach

I mapped the platform's information architecture and user flows, then built the UI on one grid and component library. I worked with front-end developers throughout on animation performance and browser support.

Six animated concepts

Each SVG was drawn and animated from scratch in SVGator for one section of the platform. SVG instead of video kept files small and sharp at any size, and each loop is timed to catch the eye without pulling attention from the content.

Final solution

One platform experience where screens, components and animations share a single visual language. Each layout puts the concept first and the density of content second.

Outcome

The design system and animations shipped across the platform and contributed to a 35% increase in engagement with visual and animated content. The SVG approach became the standard for new concept pages.

Learner dashboard

The first screen after login has to answer three questions straight away: where did I stop, what should I do next, and how far have I come? The old dashboard gave every learner the same flat layout.

Client
Log2Base2
Role
UX lead, end to end
Scope
Journey mapping, states, prototyping, handoff
Tools
Figma, Photoshop

The problem

A new learner with no history, an active learner mid-course and a returning learner all have different goals, but they saw the same static layout. Progress read like a report, not a reason to keep going.

Discovery and states

I mapped the learner journey and defined the states the dashboard had to serve, then worked with the product manager on which data each state should surface first.

Design decisions

A persistent sidebar and a main area that adapts to the learner's state. "Continue learning" is always the strongest element. Progress shows as rings and bars rather than raw numbers, and new learners get a guided path instead of an empty screen.

Final solution

A multi-state dashboard with shared components across trial videos, courses, certificates and referrals, defined for every breakpoint with the front-end team.

Outcome

The component structure and visual language from this project became the baseline for the platform's later product screens.

Video learning page

Log2Base2's learning loop is watch, solve, practise: a concept video, then a puzzle, then problems. It was spread across three pages, and learners dropped off at every hand-off. I brought it into one.

Client
Log2Base2
Role
UX lead
Scope
Flows, information architecture, UI
Tools
Adobe XD, Photoshop

The problem

Three content types with different weights and interactions (a video player, an interactive puzzle and a problem set) had to share one page without becoming cluttered, including on small screens.

Flow and structure

I mapped the user flow and organised the page as a top-to-bottom learning sequence: video first, the puzzle directly below, then practice. A progress indicator stays at the top.

Design decisions

Practice uses progressive disclosure, showing one problem at a time with a clear next step. Moving between stages needs no page load, so learners never leave the context.

Final solution

Validated with product and engineering before handoff, then used as the template for every video-based lesson on the platform.

Goal

More time on the page and fewer learners lost between watching, solving and practising.

Pricing page

Log2Base2. Adobe XD, Photoshop

The problem

Plans blurred together, feature lists were long and undifferentiated, and nothing pointed to a recommended plan. The page raised questions at the moment people were ready to pay.

Decisions

Three clearly separated tiers, a highlighted recommended plan, a short feature summary with the full comparison collapsed below, and much stronger buttons. I worked with marketing so copy and layout told the same story.

Outcome

A pricing page that is quick to scan and consistent with the rest of the design system, from discovery to purchase.

Design process

The steps flex with the project, but the order rarely does.

  1. Understand

    The business goal, the users, the constraints, and what success looks like.

  2. Map

    User journeys, states and information architecture before any pixels.

  3. Wireframe

    Low-fidelity layouts to agree on structure with product and engineering.

  4. Design and prototype

    High-fidelity screens, components and interactive prototypes in Figma or XD.

  5. Hand off and refine

    Specs, breakpoints and motion notes for developers, then review what ships.
